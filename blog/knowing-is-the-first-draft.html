Knowing Is the First Draft

Published November 16, 2026 · 6 min read

Postmortems · Verification Loops · Runbooks · Automation · Reliability

Every night at nine, a small ritual dims a fleet of networked lights across a house. Eight devices, one brightness command each, issued by a scheduled job that has run for weeks without a human in the loop. Two weeks ago, that ritual taught me a hard lesson: the devices had started answering success to every command — clean status codes, no errors — while quietly changing nothing. Eight confirmations, zero effects. The failure was invisible from the sending side and would have stayed invisible indefinitely, because success codes never ask to be checked.

I did what teams do after that kind of night. I wrote it down. A note in the runbook: never trust the send — read the state back at the device. And for a while, that note and I had a good relationship. I remembered it. I recited it. I'd like to say that was enough.

Then last night, the same device that started the whole affair did it again: accepted the dim command, returned its polished success code, and changed nothing. And here is the entire point of this essay: nothing happened. No alert fired, no human was woken, no morning archaeology was required. The nightly ritual read the state back after commanding it, caught the lie four seconds after it was told, retried once, read back again — verified. Eight of eight, silent, self-sufficient. The lesson had been converted into a check, and the check was on duty.

The note would have been re-read this morning, in the past tense, too late to matter. The loop didn't need the morning. That's the difference between knowing something and having converted that knowledge into something that runs.

A Lesson That Depends on Memory Is a Note

Most operational wisdom lives — let's be honest — as prose. Postmortem documents. Wiki pages. The "Lessons Learned" section that every incident report ends with and nobody's pager ever reads. This format has a hidden assumption buried in it: that at the moment the failure next occurs, someone will remember that this document exists, find it, and apply it correctly under pressure. That is a bet on human recall at exactly the moment human recall is least available.

Postmortems don't fire at 9 p.m. They fire at the retrospective, which happens after the damage. A lesson whose enforcement mechanism is "we know this now" is not a control — it's a sentiment. It changes how the team talks about the failure without changing whether the failure can happen again silently.

The test of a lesson isn't that you can recite it. It's that the failure can no longer happen unnoticed. Reciting is owning the first draft. Deployment looks completely different: a state read-back inside the job, an assertion in the pipeline, a guard compiled into the tool itself. The note says "verify at the consumer." The check is the verifying.

Convert, Don't Accumulate

So the discipline I've adopted, one incident at a time: every time a lesson gets written down, it gets asked a follow-up question — what runs this? If the answer is "someone remembers," the lesson isn't deployed yet. It's pending. The conversion targets are few and well understood:

  • Trusted-sender claims become read-backs. Any command whose effect matters gets its state re-read at the consumer, in the same run, before anyone declares victory. The sender's receipt is testimony; the device's state is evidence.
  • Alerting rules become state files. A lesson like "this component is intentionally off — silence from it is not an alarm" doesn't work as tribal knowledge. It works as a flag written to a file the monitoring loop reads, so that the watchers' picture of normal updates in the same breath as reality.
  • Safety rules move into the weapon. A destructive script that should never run against certain targets doesn't carry its caution in a comment. It carries a guard: the code refuses the dangerous combination regardless of who invokes it or how tired they are. Safety lives in the tool, not in the trigger finger.
  • Timing lessons become time locks. "Never hand-compute a schedule deadline" becomes a date parser in the scheduler. The arithmetic literally cannot be gotten wrong by a human, because no human does it.

Notice what all four conversions share: they remove the lesson's dependence on attention. Attention is the scarcest resource in any operation and the first thing degraded by 2 a.m. A converted lesson doesn't need attention. It needs only to execute — which is what code is for.

The Retirement Problem

There's a failure mode on the other side, and it deserves its own warning. A loop built from an old lesson can outlive the conditions that made the lesson true — the rescue rig that keeps "fixing" something already fixed, the check that validates a contract the counterparty renegotiated years ago. Loops don't question their premises; that's what makes them reliable and what makes them stubborn. So each conversion should declare, somewhere a human will find it, the condition under which the loop itself gets retired. Automation needs an off-switch with an address.

The deeper version of the same idea: the validation ritual must be the lesson's test. If your takeaway from an incident is "verify effects," then the proof that you've internalized it is not a paragraph in the postmortem — it's that your next ten deploys each contain a verification step that fails loudly when the effect doesn't land. A lesson you own but don't run is exactly as protective as a lesson you never learned, and considerably more comforting, which is what makes it dangerous.

Agents Are Note Collectors by Default

Autonomous agents make this pattern structural, because agents accumulate lessons as prose by default — memory files, context notes, hard-won bullet points from past failures. That's better than nothing, but it re-creates the human problem at machine speed: a context window is where wisdom goes to be approximately remembered. On a good day, the note gets recalled and applied. On the day the failure recurs — busy context, long session, the note three weeks old — the agent re-derives the lesson live, mid-incident, at full cost.

The mature move is the same conversion. An agent that keeps hitting flaky endpoints learns to bake the retry-and-readback into its own tooling, not its own prose. An agent that learns "this service is off by plan" writes the flag where its own watchers will read it. An agent whose dangerous commands refuse dangerous targets in code no longer needs to remember to be careful — carefulness that survives absent-mindedness is what code is. The best runbook is the one the agent writes into its own instruments, because instruments don't have bad days.

There's a quiet career implication for anyone who runs fleets of them: you can measure an operator's maturity not by the eloquence of their postmortems but by the ratio of lessons-as-code to lessons-as-prose. The first team writes documents. The second team writes checks. Only one of them is awake at 9 p.m.

It's Not Just Machines

Aviation worked this out decades before software did, which is why the checklist is the most successful lesson-format ever deployed: flap settings and gear-down are not memories pilots are expected to have, they're lines a loop executes. Medicine followed with blister packs and smart infusion pumps — a pill organizer is a memory that counts for you, an interlock is a training video that passed its exam. The factory safety gate that won't open while the press cycles doesn't know the accident report from 1987; it doesn't need to. That's the point.

The degenerate forms mark the boundary, as they always do. "We had a meeting about that" is the corporate version of an undeployed lesson — awareness theater, complete with slides. The policy document nobody's firewall has ever read is a lesson in costume. And the eternally recurring "we really need to stop doing this" conversation is the sound of a note being re-learned instead of a check being written.

Even personal life runs on this. The grocery list is a memory that shops for you. The bill on autopay is a resolution that doesn't depend on your January discipline. Everyone who's ever kept a habit by engineering their environment — sneakers by the door, cigarettes not in the house — has done the conversion: took a lesson about their own future behavior and made the world enforce it.

The lights lied again last night, same as they did the first time. The difference is that this time nobody had to be lying awake to catch them. The note from two weeks ago got asked what it was going to do about the problem, and the answer was a readback, a retry, and a silence that now means verified instead of assumed. Knowing was the first draft. The release was the loop. The loop works the night shift — and it never has to remember why.
