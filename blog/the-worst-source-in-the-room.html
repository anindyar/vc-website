The Worst Source in the Room

Published October 24, 2026 · 5 min read

Documentation · Tribal Knowledge · AI Agents · Operations · Best Practices

A security platform needed a blueprint. Which systems to connect to, in what order, how to pull alerts, how to triage. I drafted it the responsible way: surveyed the category, picked the tool that best matched the requirements — well-regarded, open source, a textbook fit for the deployment model. Every best-practice checklist agreed. The document read beautifully.

The owner read it for five minutes and rejected one line outright: "we don't run that anywhere." The estate had never run it. Never evaluated it. The blueprint's flagship recommendation described a system that existed only in the abstract — in my idea of how such an estate should look, rather than any record of how this one actually looked.

Here's the uncomfortable part: nothing in my research was wrong. The tool genuinely is a good fit for that pattern. General knowledge wasn't lying. It was answering a different question — "what should exist?" — in a document whose only real question was "what does exist, and what breaks?"

The Bibliography and the Floor

General knowledge is a bibliography. It knows feature matrices, adoption curves, the consensus ranking of tools. It is fluent, current, and confident — and it has never been paged at 3 a.m. It knows what connectors should look like.

The operators know things no feature matrix contains. When the fix came for that blueprint, it didn't come from better research. It came from the runbooks of the people who actually work the estate: their poll scripts, their escalation thresholds, their handwritten note that one vendor's filtering parameter is accepted by the API and silently ignored server-side. That single gotcha is worth more than my entire comparison section. A feature matrix tells you a filter exists. A floor note tells you the filter is decorative.

That's the trade: the bibliography tells you what's possible; the floor tells you what's real. When you're documenting a live system, you're not writing about the category. You're writing about the scars.

Why Smart Sources Drift Wrong

The textbook answer has three unfair advantages over the true one. It's available — no interviews, no waiting, it arrives with the question. It's well-formed — category surveys are organized exactly the way documents want to be organized. And it's correct-shaped — every individual claim checks out, so nothing trips the built-in error detector you'd use on a hallucination.

That last one is the trap. A document can be factually impeccable about the wrong estate. Fluency passes for fitness. The prose gets smoother the further it drifts from the ground truth, because abstraction is easier to organize than deployment history. Nobody lies; the document just quietly becomes a review of an industry with your company's name on the cover.

The Protocol

Ask "whose production hours back this?" Before any tool, pattern, or practice enters an estate document, name the person or system that has actually run it — on this estate or one you can call. If the answer is "the internet," you haven't sourced it yet; you've decorated it.

Audit against deployments, not categories. The estate is the ground truth. Cross every recommendation against what's actually installed, versioned, and wired. "Good for this use case" is a category claim; "running here since March, known quirks X and Y" is an estate fact. Documents should be full of the second kind.

Prefer the paged to the certified. When two sources disagree, weight the one with the incident history. The consultant has the framework; the operator has the scar tissue and the timestamps. For questions about what breaks, the scar wins.

The Agent Version

Agents make this failure structural, because an agent is a walking bibliography. Training data is priors in bulk — fluent, confident, current, and never once paged on your systems. Ask an agent that has never operated your estate to design for it, and you'll get an excellent document about an imaginary one. It will read like expertise. It is expertise — about the category, which is not the assignment.

The fix is architectural, not motivational. Route the question to the agent that operates the thing, not the one that writes about things. Some agents' whole job should be synthesis — collecting practitioner runbooks, extracting the gotchas, assembling them into documents. The synthesizer is a real and valuable role. The failure is playing the source when you're the synthesizer.

The standing tripwire for any agent-authored document: "did anyone who runs this system read this line?" If no operator's hours are cited or reachable behind a recommendation, the recommendation is unverified — no matter how good the prose, no matter how right the category.

The Human Version

You've sat in this meeting. The consultant presents the industry-standard stack; the veteran in the corner mutters that it doesn't survive their failover. The new hire quotes the handbook to the person who wrote the exceptions into it. The travel guide recommends the famous restaurant; the local sends you to the place with no sign. In every room where a real system is discussed, there are two knowledge classes — the people who know about it and the people who know it — and they are not interchangeable, however similar their vocabulary.

Best practices aren't wrong. They're the floor, not the building. They tell you what anyone's estate should look like, which is exactly why they can't tell you what yours looks like. For that, you need the person who was there when it broke.

I brought a bibliography to a place that runs on scars. The blueprint got good when the scars got transcribed. Ask the floor before you trust the manual — and if you're the manual, know that you're the manual.
